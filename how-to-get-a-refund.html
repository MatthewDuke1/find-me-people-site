How to get a refund (and actually get your money back)

Most refunds are lost on a deadline you didn't know about or a policy you never read. Here is the order that works: find your deadline, quote the merchant's own words, ask once and clearly in writing, then escalate.

A refund request that lands is specific and on time. A vague email sent past the window gets a canned reply and nothing else. The four steps below are the difference, and none of them require a lawyer.

1Find your deadline first

You usually have more than one clock running, and the earliest one that fits your situation is the one to plan around. Under the Fair Credit Billing Act, a credit card billing error must be disputed in writing within 60 days of the statement date the error appeared on. For a debit card or electronic fund transfer, Regulation E gives you 60 days from the statement to report the error. A card chargeback often has a wider window — network rules commonly allow around 120 days from the transaction or expected delivery date — but reason-code and issuer windows vary, so treat 120 days as an outer edge, not a plan. On top of all of that, the merchant's own return window (often 14 to 30 days) may be the tightest clock of all. Work backward from whichever fits your case.

2Read the merchant's refund policy and quote it back

Find the actual policy page — usually linked in the footer or under terms — and read what it says about your situation. If it promises a refund for a defect, a late delivery, or a cancellation inside a set window, copy that sentence and put it in your request. A company argues with a demand. It has a much harder time arguing with its own published policy.

3Send one firm, specific written request

Do it in writing so there's a record, and include everything they'd need to act without replying to ask. That means the order or transaction ID, the date, the amount, what went wrong, the remedy you want (a full refund, a partial credit, a replacement), the policy line that supports it, and a reasonable deadline to respond by. One clear message beats five frustrated ones.

4If you're ignored or refused, escalate

When the merchant stalls or says no, go to your card. File a chargeback with your issuer using the evidence you already gathered. If the charge involved a billing error or the bank won't help, you can also file a complaint with the CFPB at consumerfinance.gov/complaint. If the problem is a scam or a deceptive practice rather than a billing dispute, report it to the FTC at reportfraud.ftc.gov. For an airline, the Department of Transportation handles refund complaints.
